# RP-002

# Ontology of Redistribution Capability (R)

**Status:** Working Research Program Document

---

## Motivation

The introduction of Redistribution Capability (R) into the adaptive state representation

Y = (S, W, R)

solved the problem of observationally identical systems exhibiting different future dynamics.

However, introducing R immediately raises a deeper theoretical question.

What exactly determines Redistribution Capability?

This document summarizes the current research direction and formulates the principal hypotheses concerning the origin, formation and evolution of R.

---

# 1. The Fundamental Question

Instead of asking

"What is Redistribution Capability?"

the more fundamental question appears to be

"Who or what determines Redistribution Capability?"

This shifts the focus from mathematical representation toward ontology.

---

# 2. Three Sources of R

Current analysis suggests that Redistribution Capability is generated by three different classes of factors.

## 2.1 Natural Factors

These are not controlled by the system itself.

Examples include

- market regime;
- external liquidity;
- volatility;
- transaction costs;
- physical constraints;
- available resources.

---

## 2.2 Designed Factors

These are intentionally introduced by the architect.

Examples include

- system architecture;
- functional roles;
- redistribution rules;
- admissible transitions;
- objectives;
- strategic policy.

---

## 2.3 Emergent Factors

These arise during the lifetime of the system.

Examples include

- history;
- accumulated obligations;
- trust;
- reputation;
- statistical performance;
- previous decisions.

These cannot be completely specified during system design.

---

# 3. Governance as an Independent Source

Further analysis indicates that Redistribution Capability also depends on governance.

Different governance structures may produce different R even when

- composition,
- functional organization,
- market environment

remain identical.

---

## Strategic Governance

The architect

- defines architecture;
- assigns roles;
- establishes long-term objectives;
- determines strategic rules.

---

## Tactical Governance

A tactical participant may periodically intervene by

- changing allocations;
- extracting profit;
- modifying reserves;
- selling strategic assets;
- temporarily violating long-term policy.

Such interventions may change not only the current state but also the future space of admissible redistributions.

---

# 4. Two Types of Intervention

## Trajectory Intervention

The participant selects one of the admissible paths already allowed by the architecture.

The state changes.

The architecture remains unchanged.

---

## Structural Intervention

The participant modifies

- rules,
- objectives,
- admissible transitions,
- functional roles,
- constraints.

In this case Redistribution Capability itself changes.

---

# 5. Architectural and Actual Redistribution Capability

Current analysis suggests distinguishing two different objects.

## Architectural R

The space of possibilities defined by the original architecture.

---

## Actual R

The space of possibilities that remains after all interventions and decisions have occurred.

These two objects need not coincide.

---

# 6. Principal Hypothesis

Redistribution Capability is determined jointly by

- natural factors;
- architectural design;
- governance structure;
- accumulated history;
- realized decisions.

Consequently,

R should not be interpreted merely as another state variable.

It represents the dynamically evolving space of future redistribution possibilities generated by multiple independent sources.

---

# 7. Open Questions

Current research leaves several open questions.

1. Can Architectural R and Actual R be formally distinguished?

2. Can governance be represented as an explicit component affecting R?

3. Does every intervention necessarily reduce long-term Redistribution Capability?

4. Can the evolution of R itself become a dynamical system?

5. Can R be decomposed into strategic and tactical components?

These questions constitute one of the principal directions of future WLRT development.
